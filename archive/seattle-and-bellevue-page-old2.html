What property managers and HOA boards need to know in 2026

Washington just passed the most sweeping state-level parking reform in the country. SB 5184, signed May 2025, means new condos in Seattle and Bellevue transit corridors can now be built with zero required parking. The buildings going up in Belltown, South Lake Union, and downtown Bellevue are being designed under these rules right now. The HOA boards at those buildings will be the first to feel it.

Why Newer Buildings Have Less Guest Parking

Washington's approach to parking changed fundamentally in 2025. The question is no longer whether the state might reduce parking requirements — it already has, and by a lot.

On May 7, 2025, Governor Bob Ferguson signed SB 5184, described by housing policy analysts as "one of the most comprehensive parking reform bills enacted in any US state." Effective July 27, 2025, the law:

🚫

Bans minimums for small units

Cities cannot require any off-street parking for residential units under 1,200 sq ft — covering most urban studio and one-bedroom condos in Seattle and Bellevue.

📐

Caps minimums for larger buildings

Cities with 30,000+ residents cannot require more than 1 parking space per 2 residential units — regardless of building size or neighborhood.

🏢

Eliminates minimums for affordable and senior housing

No parking minimums allowed for affordable housing, senior housing, childcare facilities, or commercial uses in mixed-use buildings.

📅

Cities must comply by January 2027

Seattle and Bellevue are both required to update their zoning codes to match SB 5184 by January 27, 2027. Bellevue began public engagement on its code amendment in February 2026.

The result: Every new luxury condo breaking ground in a Seattle or Bellevue transit corridor from 2025 onward can legally be designed with far less parking than before — or none at all for qualifying unit types. The buildings delivering in 2027, 2028, and 2029 are being planned under these rules right now.

Seattle went further with its One Seattle Comprehensive Plan, adopted December 16, 2025. The plan creates 30 Neighborhood Centers with upzoned density across the city, and along Frequent Transit Routes, new zoning likely requires zero off-street parking. Seattle had already applied no-minimum rules to areas within 1/4 mile of frequent transit since 2012 — resulting in 18,000 fewer parking stalls built, saving $500M+ in developer costs. Those savings didn't go into guest parking.

What It Looks Like When It's Already Happening

You don't have to wait for future buildings to see the problem. It's already live in downtown Seattle.

The Emerald at 2nd & Stewart is a 40-story, 262-unit luxury condo tower in the heart of downtown. It opened with 62 parking stalls — the lowest parking ratio of any new downtown residential tower in Seattle. That's roughly one stall for every four units.

The Seattle Times documented what happened: approximately half of residents own cars, most are parking on the street, and condo brokers reported the lack of parking was "jeopardizing sales." The building did sell parking stalls — at up to $100,000 each. Those aren't HOA common elements. They're individual luxury assets. Residents who bought them aren't sharing them with guests.

The HOA at The Emerald is managing exactly the problem SpotShare solves: a building full of residents with cars, stalls priced out of reach for most HOA budgets, and guests who have nowhere to park. The only supply available is what residents aren't using at a given moment.

First Light (Belltown, parking at $90,000/stall), Graystone (First Hill, $75,000/stall), and Spire (343 units, Belltown) have all opened or are actively selling under the same conditions. Each new building in this market either solves the guest parking problem before the first resident complaint — or it doesn't, and the HOA board inherits it.

How Buildings Are Solving It Without Adding Spaces

The shared parking model starts with a counterintuitive observation: even in buildings where parking is scarce, most of the garage is empty most of the time. Residents commute by transit. They travel for work. They go weeks without moving their car. The space exists — it's just not being made available to anyone who needs it.

A shared parking program converts that idle inventory into a managed guest amenity: residents opt in to make their assigned spot available during windows they know they won't need it. Neighbors book it when a guest is coming in. The HOA doesn't need to purchase stalls at $90,000 each, negotiate with the garage, or convert common space. The supply is already in the building.

How it works in practice: A resident at The Graystone on First Hill is heading to a family event for the long weekend. She won't need her spot Thursday through Sunday. Her neighbor's parents are flying in Friday — and there's no guest parking in the building. She makes her spot available through the building app; they book it for two nights. No street parking, no $200 garage bill, no complaint to management on Monday morning.

What it needs is a system that makes the available spots bookable, handles garage access, and gives property management a real-time view of availability. Frequently Asked Questions

What did Washington's SB 5184 actually change for Seattle and Bellevue condos?

SB 5184, signed May 7, 2025 and effective July 27, 2025, is one of the most comprehensive state-level parking reform laws in the US. It prohibits cities with 30,000+ residents from requiring more than 1 parking space per 2 residential units — and eliminates parking minimums entirely for residential units under 1,200 sq ft, affordable housing, senior housing, and commercial uses in mixed-use buildings. Seattle and Bellevue must update their zoning codes to comply by January 27, 2027. Buildings already under design or construction are being planned under these new rules.

Does my Seattle or Bellevue condo building have to provide guest parking?

There is no Washington state law requiring a condo building to reserve spots specifically for guests. Whether guest parking exists, and how it's allocated, is governed by the condo declaration and HOA bylaws. Bellevue's current code requires 1 visitor stall per 20 residential units (minimum of 1), but SB 5184 is likely to limit or eliminate that requirement as the city updates its code by January 2027. For buildings already built, guest parking has always been a building decision — not a code mandate.

How much does visitor parking cost near Seattle and Bellevue condos right now?

It varies by neighborhood and time of day, but it can be steep. Evening meters in Capitol Hill's Pike-Pine corridor peak at $8.00/hour — the highest in the city. Afternoon meters in downtown Seattle and First Hill run $6.50–$7.50/hour. Monthly parking in the Seattle downtown core averages $300–$400+/month. In downtown Bellevue, monthly garages average $169, with indoor covered spaces up to $273/month. And condo parking stalls in new Seattle high-rises have sold for $75,000–$100,000 each — making them a luxury asset, not a shared amenity.

Why do newer Seattle buildings have almost no parking?

Several reasons. Seattle has already eliminated parking minimums for areas within 1/4 mile of frequent transit since 2012 — resulting in 18,000 fewer stalls built over five years. SB 5184 (effective July 2025) now limits minimums statewide. The One Seattle Comprehensive Plan (adopted December 2025) creates 30 new Neighborhood Centers where parking requirements are further reduced. And underground parking in dense urban areas is expensive to build — developers building under no-minimum rules have a strong financial incentive to build fewer spaces. The Emerald opened downtown Seattle with 62 stalls for 262 units. More buildings are following the same model.

How do HOA boards manage guest parking when the building doesn't have dedicated guest spots?

The model gaining traction in urban luxury condos is a shared resident parking program: residents who aren't using their assigned spot during a given window make it available through a building-managed platform, and their neighbors' guests book it. No new construction, no capital cost to the HOA — just a board amenity decision and a building rollout.
