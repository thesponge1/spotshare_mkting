Why Newer Buildings Have Less Guest Parking

San Diego has been systematically reducing the regulatory floor on parking supply for years. The June 2025 reform package accelerated that trajectory. The result: every new condo built in a Transit Priority Area is designed under rules that give developers maximum incentive to build fewer spaces.

San Diego's Parking Minimum Rollback Timeline

📋

January 2022 — TPA commercial reform

Ordinance O-21041 eliminated minimum parking requirements for businesses in Transit Priority Areas and commercial neighborhoods citywide. The first step in a multi-year deregulation sequence.

📅

August 22, 2025 — O-21989 amendments

Amendments to parking regulations took effect outside the Coastal Zone. Coastal Zone approval anticipated in 2026 as part of the 2026 Land Development Code Update.

🏛️

June 2, 2025 — Comprehensive reform package

City Council approved dynamic pricing, seven-day enforcement, extended meter hours to new neighborhoods (North Park, South Park, Normal Heights, College Area), and updated permit fees — accelerating the overall reduction in public parking supply.

🏢

Spring 2026 — 2026 Land Development Code Update

139 proposed amendments (107 citywide, 32 Downtown-specific) moving toward final adoption. New condo developments will be built under revised TPA parking standards that continue to reduce minimum space requirements.

The pattern is consistent: Each round of San Diego parking reform has reduced the minimum supply requirements for new development and increased the cost and scarcity of public parking. New luxury condos going up in Transit Priority Areas right now are being designed under rules where developers can build far fewer spaces than previous generations. The buildings delivering in 2027 and 2028 will have less built-in guest parking than any prior generation of downtown San Diego condos.

What HOA Boards Inherit at Delivery

California's vehicle homeowner rights add a layer of complexity. Under California Civil Code Section 4000 and related HOA statutes, residents generally have significant protections for parking in spaces designated for their use — and HOA boards face strict procedural requirements before they can fine, tow, or restrict parking. The traditional enforcement model for HOA parking management is constrained, and in buildings where guest spaces are scarce, reactive enforcement is the least effective response.

How Buildings Are Solving It Without Adding Spaces

The Logic Behind the Model

The shared parking model starts with a counterintuitive insight: even in buildings where HOA guest parking is scarce, much of the garage sits empty at any given time. Residents travel for work. They spend weekends away. They go days without moving their car. The supply exists in the building — it just isn't being made accessible to guests who need it right now.

A resident parking sharing program converts that idle inventory into a managed building amenity: residents opt in to make their assigned spot available during windows they know they won't need it. A neighbor's guest can then book it for the night, the weekend, or a few hours during a Padres game. The HOA doesn't need to purchase additional spaces, negotiate with a garage operator, or convert common areas. The supply is already in the building. Why Not Just Contract with a Nearby Garage?

The most common alternative HOA boards consider is negotiating a block of reserved spaces with a nearby parking operator. It sounds straightforward — but the math and the logistics usually work against it.

Monthly reserved parking in downtown San Diego runs $250–$400 per space. A building that wants to offer reliable guest access during Padres games and peak weekends would need multiple spaces under contract — and still has no guarantee those spaces will be available when a guest needs them, since reserved blocks aren't always exclusive. The PM also becomes the coordinator: how does a guest claim a space? What happens when the garage is full despite the contract? Who handles the billing dispute when a guest is turned away?

The shared model sidesteps all of this. The building isn't negotiating a lease, paying a monthly infrastructure cost, or routing guests through a third-party operator in an unfamiliar part of the neighborhood. The supply is in the building's own garage. The reservation is made before the guest leaves home. And the PM gets an audit trail without fielding individual requests.

San Diego's regulatory trajectory makes the case for this model concrete. As the city's 2026 Land Development Code Update reduces parking minimums further and dynamic meter pricing makes street alternatives more expensive, buildings that have activated their idle resident-assigned inventory become measurably more competitive for prospective buyers and residents. A managed guest parking system is no longer a nice-to-have parking amenity for condos — it's the gap between a building that works and one that generates chronic complaints.

What a Modern HOA Guest Parking System Needs

Visitor parking management in a condo building is a different problem than managing a public garage or a surface lot. The right system has to work within HOA governance constraints, integrate with existing building infrastructure, and serve residents who have varying schedules and no obligation to participate. Before evaluating any solution, these are the criteria that matter.

🔑

Works with existing garage access

No proprietary hardware mandate. The system should integrate with the building's existing gate, fob, or access control infrastructure — not require a full capital replacement as a condition of adoption.

🏗️

No new construction required

The supply is already in the building. A guest parking solution should activate what's already there — not create a capital project or require lease negotiations with a third-party garage operator.

✋

Resident-controlled opt-in

Individual residents decide whether to share their assigned space and during which windows. The HOA board sets the framework; residents make the decision for their own spot. Mandated participation is a governance minefield.

📱

Mobile-accessible reservation for guests

Guests should be able to book a space in advance from their phone. Showing up to an unmarked garage hoping a space is available is not a guest experience — it's a liability waiting to happen.

📊

Property manager dashboard

The PM needs an audit trail, a mechanism for dispute resolution, and utilization visibility — without fielding individual resident requests manually. If the system creates more PM workload, it hasn't solved the problem.

⚡

Handles event-surge demand

In a city with $10/hour event zone pricing, guest parking demand spikes predictably around Padres games, concerts, and conventions. The system needs to accommodate those windows without a manual scramble every time the schedule fills up.

How HOA Boards Should Evaluate a Guest Parking Solution

Understanding the shared parking model is one thing. Getting a board to a decision is another. Here is what the evaluation process typically looks like for a downtown San Diego HOA — and what to confirm before bringing a solution to a vote.

Does the Board Need a Vote?

Most HOA decisions of this type require a simple majority board vote — not a member vote or CC&R amendment. Under California Civil Code Section 4000 and related HOA statutes, implementing a new building amenity or service policy generally falls within board authority without requiring a full membership vote. That said, the CC&Rs govern. Boards should review their declaration before assuming a vote threshold — and confirm with HOA counsel if the declaration restricts how guest parking is managed.

Does the CC&R Need to Be Amended?

In most buildings, activating an opt-in resident sharing program does not require a CC&R amendment. It is managed as an HOA service policy, not a property rights change — residents retain full control of their assigned space and participate voluntarily. Amendment requirements typically only arise if the existing declaration explicitly restricts access to assigned spaces or prohibits sharing arrangements. Confirm with HOA counsel if your declaration has specific language around parking use.

What Does Implementation Require?

Three things: an access control integration assessment, a resident communication plan, and a PM dashboard setup. None of them require new construction, hardware replacement, or a garage operator contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much did San Diego parking rates change in 2025?

Significantly. Street meter rates doubled from $1.25 to $2.50/hour on January 31, 2025, and enforcement now runs 8am–10pm seven days a week including Sundays. The city also activated $10/hour event zone pricing across 200+ blocks in East Village, Gaslamp, and Marina District for Padres games and large concerts. Balboa Park added paid parking (up to $16/day for non-residents) in early 2026. The City Council approved a comprehensive parking reform package in June 2025 that includes dynamic pricing — rates in high-demand areas will rise further as that rolls out. Current projections put base meter rates at $3.00–$3.50/hour by late 2026 or 2027.

Why are newer downtown San Diego condos being built with less parking?

San Diego's 2022 Transit Priority Area ordinance (O-21041) eliminated minimum parking requirements for commercial properties in TPAs. Subsequent amendments (O-21989, effective August 2025) extended those changes. The 2026 Land Development Code Update — with 139 proposed amendments including 32 Downtown-specific ones — is moving toward final adoption in spring 2026 and will further reduce minimum requirements for residential development in transit-served areas. For developers, each parking space in a downtown high-rise costs significant capital to build and operate. With no regulatory floor requiring them to build guest spaces, the default decision is to build fewer. HOA boards at new buildings inherit whatever parking ratio the developer chose — and there is no guarantee guest spaces were part of that calculation.

How do HOA buildings manage guest parking without adding spaces?

The most effective approach to HOA parking management in buildings with limited dedicated guest spaces is activating the parking supply that already exists. In any condo building with resident-assigned spaces, a portion of those spaces are unused at any given time — residents are traveling, at work, or simply haven't driven in days. A shared parking program creates a system for residents to make their assigned spaces available to guests during those windows. Guests reserve a space in advance through the building system, and the HOA manages the workflow without adding spaces, hiring staff, or contracting with a garage operator. This model works specifically because every assigned space in the building is a potential guest space during the hours its resident doesn't need it.

Does a downtown San Diego condo building have to provide guest parking?

There is no California state law requiring a condo building to designate dedicated guest parking spaces. Whether guest parking exists, how many spaces are designated, and how they are managed is governed by the condo declaration and HOA bylaws. San Diego's TPA parking reforms mean new developments in transit corridors are not required to build dedicated parking at any minimum ratio — guest spaces are whatever the developer decided to build and whatever the HOA decides to manage. Many older downtown buildings have a small number of designated guest spaces; newer buildings under current rules may have fewer or none as a built-in feature.

Why do buildings with assigned parking still have a guest parking problem?

Because having total parking spaces is different from having accessible guest parking at any given moment. In a building where every space is assigned to a specific resident, those spaces are controlled by individual residents — not by the building. A guest cannot simply park in any open-looking space without risking a tow. Without a system to identify which resident-assigned spaces are temporarily available and make them bookable, the building's total parking capacity exists on paper but is inaccessible to guests in practice. That is the gap a shared parking program fills — not by adding spaces, but by making the existing inventory visible and bookable.

How long does it take for a downtown San Diego building to activate a shared guest parking system?

For most buildings, the timeline from board decision to live reservations is four to six weeks. The board assessment — reviewing current parking allocation and confirming access control compatibility — typically takes two to four weeks. The board vote follows on whatever meeting schedule is in place. Access control integration (connecting to the building's existing gate and fob infrastructure) takes one to three weeks, with no hardware replacement required for supported systems. The resident opt-in campaign runs parallel or immediately after — and because participation is voluntary, the first group of residents who enroll defines the initial guest inventory. The property manager gets dashboard access at go-live. The board decision is usually the longest variable; the technical and operational steps move quickly once it's made.

What do residents think about sharing their assigned parking spot with guests?

Participation is voluntary — no resident is required to share their assigned space. That structure is critical: mandated participation in any HOA amenity is a governance problem, and mandating that a resident make their assigned parking spot available to others would almost certainly conflict with their property rights under the declaration. What typically happens in practice is that residents who travel frequently, work from home and don't drive daily, or simply want to reduce their HOA-related costs opt in readily. Residents who use their spot daily, have an EV with specific charging access needs, or have other reasons for regular use simply don't participate. The building's guest inventory is defined by the residents who choose to share — and it grows as more residents recognize the convenience and benefit of the system.

How does a building handle a parking dispute — for example, if a guest damages a space or doesn't vacate on time?

This is one of the primary reasons a managed system is meaningfully better than an informal arrangement. When guest parking is managed through an informal call or text chain, the property manager has no record of who was in a space, when they arrived, or when they were supposed to leave. Disputes are resolved by whoever makes the most compelling argument. A managed system gives the property manager an audit trail: reservation timestamps, the guest's contact information, vehicle details, and access logs tied to the reservation. If a guest overstays, the PM can reach them directly. If there's damage, the reservation record establishes who was in the space and when. The PM is not adjudicating disputes on the honor system — there is a documented record behind every guest parking event.

What should HOA boards ask about access control integration when evaluating a guest parking system?

The most important question is whether the system integrates with what the building already has — not whether it requires replacing it. Downtown San Diego luxury high-rises commonly use systems including LiftMaster, ButterflyMX, PDK, and similar access control platforms. A shared guest parking system should be able to connect to the building's existing gate and fob infrastructure so guests can be granted access for their reserved window without a new hardware installation. Boards should ask any vendor to confirm compatibility during an assessment phase before making a commitment. If a building's current system is not supported, that should surface before adoption — not after. No proprietary hardware mandate should be a condition of getting started.

The HOA parking software market has expanded significantly in 2025–2026, with new entrants including SmartLotIQ (ALPR gate control), R2Park (digital registration), and Reliant Parking (permit management) all targeting the same HOA search queries. The distinction that matters most is whether a vendor is selling enforcement infrastructure — permits, citations, towing integrations — or a resident-driven sharing amenity. Those are different products solving different problems, and conflating them in an evaluation process leads to buying the wrong solution for your building's actual complaint pattern.
