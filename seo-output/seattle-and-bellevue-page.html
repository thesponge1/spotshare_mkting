What HOA boards and property managers need to know about the visitor parking problem — and how buildings are solving it

Who This Affects

The guest parking problem in Seattle and Bellevue condos looks different depending on your role in the building. Here's what's at stake for each stakeholder.

🏛️

HOA Boards

Under Washington's Condominium Act (RCW 64.34), HOA boards have general authority to implement parking service policies by board vote — but the infrastructure to back up that policy has to be chosen. SB 5184 means any building delivered after July 27, 2025 may have been built with 0.5 stalls per unit or fewer, and no guest-specific allocation is required by law. Boards at these buildings are inheriting parking scarcity as a design feature, not an oversight. Guest parking disputes are among the most common complaint categories in Pacific Northwest condo management, and without infrastructure behind the policy, the board absorbs every one of them.

Why Newer Buildings Have Less Guest Parking

Two overlapping regulatory changes — one already law, one pending — have fundamentally altered the Seattle and Bellevue condo parking landscape. Washington's SB 5184 is the most sweeping state-level parking reform in the country. Bellevue may go further still.

SB 5184 — Washington's Landmark Parking Reform

📋

Signed May 7, 2025 — Effective July 27, 2025

SB 5184 caps parking for new multifamily development in transit corridors at 0.5 stalls per unit. That means a 143-unit building like Bosa Park Row can be built with as few as 72 total stalls — for residents and guests combined — and be fully compliant. No guest-specific allocation is required. The Sightline Institute called it the most comprehensive state-level parking reform law in the United States.

📅

One Year In — Already Producing Results

Sightline Institute's May 7, 2026 report confirmed SB 5184 is working as intended: Vancouver, WA converted a city parking lot to 95 affordable homes; Bothell and Federal Way are accelerating their own reform; WA Dept. of Commerce is finalizing implementation guidance by June 2026. Cities with populations over 50,000 — including Bellevue — have until January 27, 2027 to comply. Many are moving early.

🏗️

What 0.5 Stalls Per Unit Means in Practice

A 262-unit building built at the 0.5 cap delivers with 131 total stalls — for all residents and all guests. The Emerald (262 units, 62 stalls) is already operating at a 1:4.2 resident-to-stall ratio and the Seattle Times documented that brokers there cited parking as "jeopardizing sales" with half of residents parking on-street. Buildings delivering after July 2025 face the same math by design, not by accident.

🏛️

Seattle One Seattle Plan — January 29, 2026

Seattle's Centers & Corridors legislation, adopted January 29, 2026, upzoned 30 Neighborhood Centers and added parking exemptions for affordable housing, homes under 1,200 sq ft, and mixed-use ground floors. The policy direction is consistent: every recent legislative action in Seattle reduces the amount of parking new development is required to include. HOA boards at buildings delivered into this environment inherit whatever the developer chose to build — not a regulated minimum.

Bellevue's Next Step — Potentially Eliminating All Minimums

The Urbanist reported January 20, 2026: Bellevue is actively weighing going beyond SB 5184 compliance to eliminate all parking minimums citywide — following Spokane, Shoreline, and Bremerton. The BelRed Code Update and 2026 Omnibus Code Amendments are both advancing with parking policy central. Separately, FOX 13 reported Bellevue is considering paid street parking for downtown — potentially removing the last free fallback for building guests. If both policies advance, Bellevue condo guest parking goes from scarce to entirely discretionary by design.

The math behind Bellevue's current visitor minimum: Bellevue's Land Use Code 20.25A.080 requires exactly 1 visitor stall per 20 residential units — the code minimum. For Bosa Park Row's 143 units, that's 7 required visitor stalls. For Main & Bellevue's 449 units, that's 22. If Bellevue drops all minimums, even those numbers become optional for future developments. The buildings delivering in 2027 and beyond will have whatever guest parking the developer decided to include — which, under zero-minimum rules, will generally be less than any prior generation of Bellevue condos.

How Buildings Are Solving It Without Adding Spaces

The Logic Behind the Model

The shared parking model begins with a counterintuitive observation: even in buildings where HOA guest parking is essentially nonexistent, much of the garage sits empty on any given day. Residents work from home and leave their car for days. They fly out of SeaTac for a long weekend. They take the Link Light Rail downtown and don't drive back until Friday. The supply already exists in the building — it simply isn't accessible to guests who need it right now.

A resident parking sharing program converts that idle inventory into a managed building amenity. Residents opt in to make their assigned spot available during windows they know they won't need it. A neighbor's guest can then book it in advance — for an overnight stay, a weekend visit, or an evening in Capitol Hill when street parking at $8.00/hour isn't a realistic option. Learn more about how the shared parking model works at the building level. The HOA doesn't need to lease new spaces, invest in construction, or negotiate with a garage operator. The supply is already in the building.

With a shared parking system: That same resident is flying to San Francisco for four days. She won't be using her assigned space while she's gone. Why Not Just Use a Nearby Garage?

The most common alternative HOA boards consider is negotiating reserved space blocks with a nearby parking operator. In Seattle and Bellevue's market, that math works against the building.

Monthly reserved parking in downtown Seattle runs $300–$400+ per space. A building wanting reliable guest access on event weekends, holiday visits, or peak tourism season would need multiple spaces under contract — and still faces the same coordination problem: how does a guest claim a specific space? What happens when the garage is full despite the building's contract? Who handles the dispute? The PM becomes the coordinator for a third-party facility the building doesn't control.

The shared model sidesteps the lease, the monthly cost, and the coordination overhead entirely. The supply is in the building's own garage, under its own access control. The reservation is made before the guest leaves Bellevue. The property manager gets an audit trail without fielding individual requests for each visit. And for buildings like Park Row, where concierge valet is positioned as a signature amenity, having a managed system behind that positioning is what makes the amenity real.

What a Modern HOA Guest Parking System Needs

Visitor parking management in a Seattle or Bellevue condo building operates under a specific set of constraints: buildings constructed under SB 5184's 0.5 stall cap, Bellevue's 1-per-20-units visitor minimum, existing access control infrastructure, and residents with varying schedules and no obligation to participate. Before evaluating any condo guest parking solution, these are the criteria that matter.

🔑

Works with existing garage access

No proprietary hardware mandate. The system should integrate with the building's existing gate, fob, or access control infrastructure — not require a capital replacement project as a condition of adoption. Buildings in Seattle and Bellevue commonly use a range of access control systems; compatibility should be confirmed during assessment, not assumed.

🏗️

No new construction required

Under SB 5184, underground parking in Pacific Northwest developments costs far more per stall than the revenue it captures. A guest parking solution should activate what's already there — not create a capital project. The shared model works precisely because the supply already exists in every building with resident-assigned spaces.

✋

Resident-controlled opt-in

Individual residents decide whether to share their assigned space and during which windows. The HOA board sets the framework; residents make the decision for their own spot. Under Washington's Condominium Act (RCW 64.34), a resident's assigned parking space is a property right — any system that mandates sharing or removes resident control creates a governance problem before it solves a parking one.

📱

Mobile-accessible reservation for guests

Guests should be able to book a space in advance from their phone. In a market where street alternatives run $8.00/hour in Capitol Hill and a building's RPZ permit cap can be exhausted before December, the ability to offer a confirmed, pre-booked space is a meaningful amenity — not a fallback. A building parking amenity that requires guests to show up and hope is not a system.

📊

Property manager dashboard with audit trail

HOA parking management software should reduce PM workload, not add to it. The PM needs visibility into reservations, a dispute resolution mechanism, and the ability to reference a documented record. Every parking dispute under a managed system has a timestamp, a guest contact, and an access log. 📋

Scales with the building's amenity positioning

How HOA Boards Should Evaluate a Guest Parking Solution

Understanding the problem and understanding the shared parking model are two separate steps from getting a Washington condo board to a decision. Here is what the evaluation process typically looks like — and what to confirm before bringing a solution to a vote under Washington HOA governance.

Does the Board Need a Vote?

Under Washington's Condominium Act (RCW 64.34.304), the HOA board has general authority to implement building service policies — including guest parking systems — through board vote rather than a member vote or declaration amendment, provided the CC&Rs do not restrict it. That said, the declaration governs. Boards should review their CC&Rs before assuming a vote threshold, and confirm with HOA counsel if the declaration contains specific language about parking use or sharing arrangements. Washington HOA parking management decisions made through proper board process are generally straightforward — the governance complexity usually comes from skipping the review step, not from the statute itself.

Does the CC&R Need to Be Amended?

In most buildings, activating an opt-in resident sharing program does not require a CC&R amendment. It is managed as an HOA service policy — residents retain full control of their assigned space and participate voluntarily. Amendment requirements typically arise only if the existing declaration explicitly restricts access to assigned spaces or prohibits sharing arrangements. Confirm with HOA counsel if your declaration has specific restrictive language; most Washington condo declarations treat a managed guest parking system as a board service policy, not a rights change requiring declaration amendment.

What Does Implementation Require?

Three things: an access control integration assessment, a resident communication and opt-in plan, and a PM dashboard setup. None require new construction, hardware replacement, or a garage operator contract.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is SB 5184 and what does it mean for Seattle and Bellevue condos?

SB 5184 is Washington State's landmark parking reform law, signed May 7, 2025 and effective July 27, 2025. It caps parking for new multifamily development in transit corridors at 0.5 stalls per unit — meaning a 200-unit building can be built with as few as 100 total stalls for residents and guests combined, and be fully compliant. The Sightline Institute called it the most comprehensive state-level parking reform in the country. For HOA boards at buildings delivered after the effective date, the guest parking they inherit is whatever the developer chose to build under these rules — no regulatory minimum guarantees guest spaces exist at all. Cities with populations over 50,000 have until January 27, 2027 to comply, and many including Bellevue are moving early.

Why are newer Bellevue condos being built with so little visitor parking?

Two reasons. First, Bellevue's Land Use Code (20.25A.080) requires only 1 visitor stall per 20 residential units as its minimum — which means a 143-unit building like Bosa Park Row must provide only 7 designated visitor stalls by code. Second, SB 5184 caps total parking (resident + guest combined) at 0.5 stalls per unit for new multifamily in transit corridors. Under that cap, there is no specific guest allocation at all — the developer decides the ratio. Both rules point in the same direction: less guest parking with each new building that delivers, not more. If Bellevue drops all parking minimums citywide as currently under consideration, even the 1-per-20 baseline becomes discretionary.

How do HOA buildings manage guest parking without adding spaces?

The most effective approach in buildings with limited or no dedicated guest spaces is activating the parking supply that already exists. In any condo building with resident-assigned spaces, a meaningful share of those spaces are unused at any given time — residents are traveling, commuting by transit, or working remotely without driving. A shared parking program creates a system for residents to make their assigned spaces available to guests during windows they're not needed. Guests reserve a confirmed space in advance through the building system; the HOA manages the workflow without adding spaces, hiring staff, or contracting with a garage operator. The model works because every resident-assigned space in the building is a potential guest space during the hours its owner doesn't need it.

Does a Seattle or Bellevue condo building have to provide guest parking?

There is no Washington state law requiring a condo building to designate dedicated guest parking spaces. Bellevue's Land Use Code requires only 1 visitor stall per 20 units as a minimum for new construction — and that minimum may be eliminated entirely if Bellevue's current reform proposals advance. In Seattle, there is no city-wide minimum for visitor parking in residential buildings near transit. SB 5184 caps total stalls at 0.5 per unit but does not mandate any guest-specific allocation. Whether guest parking exists, how many spaces are designated, and how they are managed is governed by the condo declaration and HOA bylaws — and by whatever the developer chose to build.

Why do buildings with assigned parking still have a guest parking problem?

Because total parking spaces in the building is different from parking that guests can access at any given moment. In a building where every space is assigned to a specific resident, those spaces are controlled by individual residents — not by the building. A guest cannot park in an open-looking assigned space without risking a tow. Without a system to identify which resident-assigned spaces are temporarily available and make them bookable, the building's total parking capacity exists on paper but is inaccessible to guests in practice. The Emerald (262 units, 62 stalls) documented this precisely — brokers reported parking was "jeopardizing sales" with half of residents parking on-street, despite the building having a garage.

How long does it take for a building to activate a shared guest parking system?

For most buildings, the timeline from board decision to live reservations is four to six weeks. The board assessment — reviewing current parking allocation and confirming access control compatibility — typically takes two to four weeks. The board vote follows on whatever meeting schedule is in place. Access control integration (connecting to the building's existing gate and fob infrastructure) takes one to three weeks, with no hardware replacement required for supported systems. The resident opt-in campaign runs parallel or immediately after — because participation is voluntary, the first group of residents who enroll defines the initial guest inventory. The board decision is usually the longest variable; the technical steps move quickly once it's made.

What do residents think about sharing their assigned parking spot?

Participation is voluntary — no resident is required to share their assigned space. Under Washington's Condominium Act (RCW 64.34), an assigned parking space is generally a property right of the unit owner; any system that mandated sharing would create a governance conflict rather than solve a parking problem. In practice, residents who travel frequently, commute by transit, or work from home without driving regularly tend to opt in readily. Residents who use their spot daily simply don't participate. The building's guest parking inventory is defined by the residents who choose to share — and it grows as more residents recognize the benefit.

How does the building handle a parking dispute if a guest damages a space or doesn't leave on time?

This is one of the primary reasons a managed system is meaningfully better than an informal arrangement. When guest parking is handled through an informal text chain or verbal permission, the property manager has no record of who was in a space, when they arrived, or when they were supposed to leave. A managed system gives the property manager an audit trail: reservation timestamps, guest contact information, vehicle details, and access logs tied to the reservation. If a guest overstays, the PM can reach them directly using the reservation record. If there's damage, the same record establishes who was in the space and when — the PM is not adjudicating on the honor system, and the board has documentation if the dispute escalates.

What should HOA boards ask about access control integration when evaluating a guest parking system?

The most important question is whether the system integrates with what the building already has — not whether it requires replacing it. Seattle and Bellevue luxury high-rises use a range of access control systems. A shared guest parking system should be able to connect to the building's existing gate and access infrastructure so guests can be granted access for their reserved window without a new hardware installation. Boards should ask any vendor to confirm compatibility during an assessment phase before making a commitment. If a building's current access system is not supported, that should surface in the assessment — not after adoption. No hardware mandate should be a condition of getting started.
